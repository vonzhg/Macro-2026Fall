\begin{frame}{Two Issues, and They Are Separable}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
\small
\textbf{1. Which basis $\{\psi_i\}$?}
\medskip
\begin{itemize}\small
  \item A \textbf{global} basis --- each $\psi_i$ is non-zero and smooth almost everywhere on the domain $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{spectral methods}. Chebyshev polynomials, Fourier series.
  \item A \textbf{local} basis --- each $\psi_i$ is non-zero only on a small piece of the domain $\Rightarrow$ \textbf{finite element methods}. Tent functions, splines.
\end{itemize}
\medskip
\textbf{2. How do we ``project''?} Collocation, Galerkin, least squares, \dots\ --- 5.B5.
\medskip

The two choices are independent, and the published methods are just particular pairs.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
\begin{conceptbox}[title=\textbf{The trade}]\small
A global basis converges fast \emph{if the function is smooth} and degrades badly if it is not. A local basis is indifferent to smoothness and converges slowly.
\end{conceptbox}
\medskip
\begin{examplebox}\footnotesize
And they mix: spectral elements use a high-order basis \emph{within} each element. So does a deep network, in effect.
\end{examplebox}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vspace{1mm}
\src{Quant\_Macro Lec.~9, ``Two issues'' and ``Spectral methods''. Main reference: Judd (1992), \emph{JET} 58.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Obvious Basis, and Why It Fails}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
\small
Start with monomials: $\;1,\;x,\;x^{2},\;x^{3},\dots$
\medskip

\textbf{The theory is reassuring.} By Stone--Weierstrass, every continuous function on a closed interval $[a,b]$ can be approximated \emph{uniformly}, as closely as you like, by a polynomial. Nothing is missing from the family.
\medskip

\textbf{The practice is not.} Compare the graphs of $x^{10}$ and $x^{11}$ on $[0,1]$: they are nearly the same function. As basis directions they are $\mathbf{2.6^{\circ}}$ apart, so asking how much of each a fit needs is asking a question the data can barely answer.
\medskip

And monomials differ enormously in \textbf{size} across the domain, so their coefficients do too, and rounding errors accumulate.
\end{column}

\begin{frame}{Where That Matrix Comes From}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
\small
Fit $f$ on $[0,1]$ with $\;p(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}\theta_j x^{j}$, choosing coefficients to minimise the
squared error:
\[
  \min_{\theta}\;\int_0^1\Big(f(x)-\sum_{j}\theta_j x^{j}\Big)^{2}dx .
\]
Differentiate with respect to $\theta_i$, set to zero --- one equation per coefficient:
\begin{align*}
  \int_0^1 x^{i}\Big(f(x)-\sum_{j}\theta_j x^{j}\Big)dx &= 0\\[1mm]
  \sum_{j}\theta_j\underbrace{\int_0^1 x^{i}x^{j}dx}_{\textstyle H_{ij}}
   &= \underbrace{\int_0^1 x^{i}f(x)\,dx}_{\textstyle b_i}
\end{align*}
That is $\;H\theta=b$: a linear system in the $n+1$ unknown coefficients.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
\begin{conceptbox}[title=\textbf{What $H$ and $b$ hold}]\footnotesize
$H_{ij}$ is the \textbf{overlap of basis function $i$ with basis function $j$}; $b_i$ is its overlap
with the \emph{target}. The system says: give the approximation the same overlap with every basis
function that $f$ has.
\end{conceptbox}
\medskip
\begin{examplebox}\footnotesize
For monomials the integral is immediate:
\[
  H_{ij}=\int_0^1 x^{i+j}dx=\frac{1}{i+j+1},
\]
which is the \textbf{Hilbert matrix} --- the textbook example of an ill-conditioned system. Not an
analogy: the same matrix.
\end{examplebox}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vspace{1mm}
\src{$b$ depends on the function being fitted; $H$ does not. Everything that follows is about $H$ alone, so it is a property of \emph{the basis}, not of the problem.}
\end{frame}

%=============================================================================
\begin{frame}{What Goes Wrong: a Nearly Singular System}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
\small
Nothing \emph{visible} goes wrong: the solve succeeds and hands back $n+1$ numbers. They are simply
not pinned down.
\medskip

Because $x^{10}$ and $x^{11}$ are almost the same function, raising $\theta_{10}$ and lowering
$\theta_{11}$ by a similar amount leaves the fitted polynomial almost unchanged. So \textbf{very
different coefficient vectors fit $f$ about equally well}, and the fit cannot choose between them.
\medskip

That is \textbf{collinear regressors}: fitted values fine, individual coefficients unidentified.
\medskip

\textbf{And the analogy holds all the way.} At degree $13$ the recovered coefficients are wrong by
$180\%$ while the fitted \emph{polynomial} still matches to $2\times10^{-8}$. What is destroyed is
$\theta$ --- exactly what a projection method solves for and carries around.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.45\textwidth}
\begin{conceptbox}[title=\textbf{$H$ is a table of angles}]\footnotesize
Normalise an entry by its diagonal neighbours and you get the \textbf{cosine of the angle} between
two basis functions:
\[
  \frac{H_{ij}}{\sqrt{H_{ii}H_{jj}}}=\cos\angle(x^{i},x^{j}),
\]
and they collapse together as the degree rises --- which is why the trouble grows with $n$.
\end{conceptbox}
\medskip
\begin{center}\footnotesize
\begin{tabularx}{\linewidth}{@{}>{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X r r@{}}
\toprule
pair & $\cos$ & angle \\
\midrule
$x^{1},x^{2}$ & $0.9682$ & $14.5^{\circ}$ \\
$x^{5},x^{6}$ & $0.9965$ & $4.8^{\circ}$ \\
$x^{10},x^{11}$ & $0.9990$ & $\mathbf{2.6^{\circ}}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\begin{frame}{Measured: How Much It Costs}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
\small
\textbf{$\mathrm{cond}(H)$ is an amplification factor.} Nudge the right-hand side and the solution
can move by that much more:
\[
  \frac{\|\Delta\theta\|}{\|\theta\|}\;\le\;\mathrm{cond}(H)\,\frac{\|\Delta b\|}{\|b\|} .
\]
You cannot avoid nudging it: merely \emph{storing} $b$ rounds it, by about one machine epsilon,
$2.2\times10^{-16}$. So $\mathrm{cond}(H)\times2.2\times10^{-16}$ is the worst case for the answer,
whatever solver you use.
\medskip

It is a \textbf{worst case}, not a forecast --- so we also recovered a known $\theta$ and measured
what actually comes back.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.51\textwidth}
\begin{center}\footnotesize

\begin{frame}{Where the Other Matrix Comes From}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
\small
Least squares was one way to pin down $\theta$. The other is \textbf{interpolation}: pick $n+1$ nodes
and demand that the approximation match $f$ \emph{exactly} there,
\[
  p(x_i)=f(x_i),\qquad i=0,\dots,n .
\]
Write $p$ out in the basis and that is $n+1$ linear equations:
\[
  \sum_{j=0}^{n}\theta_j\,\underbrace{\psi_j(x_i)}_{\textstyle V_{ij}}
  \;=\;\underbrace{f(x_i)}_{\textstyle f_i}
\]
so $\;V\theta=f$, where \textbf{$V_{ij}$ is basis function $j$ evaluated at node $i$}. With monomials
$\psi_j(x)=x^{j}$ that is $V_{ij}=x_i^{\,j}$ --- the \textbf{Vandermonde matrix}.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
\begin{conceptbox}[title=\textbf{Two matrices, one question}]\footnotesize
$H$ was built from \textbf{integrals} --- how much two basis functions overlap across the domain.
$V$ is built from \textbf{evaluations} --- what each is worth at a point. Both end in a linear system
for $\theta$, and both inherit the basis's conditioning.
\end{conceptbox}
\medskip
\begin{examplebox}[title=\textbf{$V$ hides two choices}]\footnotesize
\textbf{Where} you evaluate (the nodes) and \textbf{which} basis. They are separable, and it is worth
knowing which does the work. $V$ is also the matrix every collocation method in 5.B5--5.B6 forms.
\end{examplebox}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vspace{1mm}
\src{Nodes below are the $n$ Chebyshev points on $[-1,1]$ in both columns --- \textbf{only the basis changes}, so anything that differs is the basis's doing.}
\end{frame}

\begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
\begin{takeawaybox}\footnotesize
Below degree $\sim20$ none of this matters --- which is why monomials are survivable on a small
problem. By degree $41$ the monomial fit is worthless and the Chebyshev one has not moved. \textbf{It
is what licenses the rest of the block:} 5.B3 expands to degree $33$, 5.B6 solves at degree $11$.
\end{takeawaybox}
\medskip
\begin{conceptbox}[title=\textbf{Why it never degrades}]\footnotesize
At the Chebyshev roots the columns of $V$ are orthogonal, so
$V^{\!\top}V=\mathrm{diag}(n,\tfrac n2,\dots,\tfrac n2)$, measured to $2\times10^{-14}$ off the
diagonal. Hence $\mathrm{cond}(V)=\sqrt{n/(n/2)}=\sqrt2$ \emph{at every} $n$.
\end{conceptbox}
\end{column}
\end{columns}

\begin{frame}{Why Orthogonality Helps, in Four Words Each}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.53\textwidth}
\small
\begin{itemize}\small\setlength{\itemsep}{1.5mm}
  \item \textbf{Simplicity.} Orthogonality means $H_{ij}=0$ off the diagonal, so $H\theta=b$ \textbf{decouples}: the coordinates of a function \emph{are} its projections onto the basis, and there is no system left to solve.
  \item \textbf{Independence.} Orthogonal functions are linearly independent by construction, so each one adds a genuinely new direction.
  \item \textbf{Numerical stability.} Small changes in the data produce small changes in the coefficients --- which is exactly what the previous slide measured.
  \item \textbf{Interpretability.} Each coefficient means something on its own. This is the same property that makes principal components readable.
\end{itemize}
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
\begin{conceptbox}[title=\textbf{The formula}]\small
For $x=\sum_j c_j v_j$ with $\langle v_i,v_j\rangle=0$ for $i\neq j$, take inner products with $v_i$: every term vanishes but one, leaving
\[
  c_i=\frac{\langle x,v_i\rangle}{\|v_i\|^{2}},
\]
and simply $c_i=\langle x,v_i\rangle$ if the basis is orthonormal.
\end{conceptbox}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vspace{1mm}
\src{Quant\_Macro Lec.~9, ``Orthogonal Basis'' and ``Projections in an Orthogonal Basis''.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The Same Thing in $\mathbb{R}^{2}$}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.53\textwidth}
\small
Take $x=(3,4)$ and the orthonormal basis
\[
  v_1=\Big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\Big),\qquad
  v_2=\Big(\tfrac{-1}{\sqrt2},\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\Big).
\]
The coordinates are inner products, nothing more:
\begin{align*}
  c_1&=\langle x,v_1\rangle=\tfrac{3}{\sqrt2}+\tfrac{4}{\sqrt2}=\tfrac{7}{\sqrt2},\\
  c_2&=\langle x,v_2\rangle=-\tfrac{3}{\sqrt2}+\tfrac{4}{\sqrt2}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2},
\end{align*}
so that $x=\tfrac{7}{\sqrt2}v_1+\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}v_2$.
\medskip

In a non-orthogonal basis those two numbers would require solving a $2\times2$ system --- and in a badly conditioned basis of degree $13$, a system with no correct digits.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.44\textwidth}
\begin{takeawaybox}\footnotesize
Function approximation is this, with $\langle f,g\rangle=\int f g\,w$ in place of the dot product. Nothing else changes.
\end{takeawaybox}
\medskip
\begin{examplebox}\footnotesize
Which is why Galerkin (5.B5) is natural: ``make the residual orthogonal to every basis function'' means ``the residual has no component we could still represent''.
\end{examplebox}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vspace{1mm}
\src{Quant\_Macro Lec.~9, ``Projections in an Orthogonal Basis'' (worked example frame).}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Trigonometric Series}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
\small
The other classical orthogonal family:
\[
  \tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}},\;\tfrac{\cos x}{\sqrt{2\pi}},\;\tfrac{\sin x}{\sqrt{2\pi}},\;\dots,
  \tfrac{\cos kx}{\sqrt{2\pi}},\;\tfrac{\sin kx}{\sqrt{2\pi}},\dots
\]
\medskip
It is the right basis for \textbf{periodic} functions, and essentially nothing in economics is periodic. A capital stock does not return to where it started after $2\pi$.
\medskip

Forcing a periodic basis onto a non-periodic function creates an artificial discontinuity at the ends of the interval --- and with it the \textbf{Gibbs phenomenon}, which drags the convergence rate down to $O(1/n)$: the error falls like one over the number of terms, which is very slow.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.43\textwidth}
\begin{examplebox}\footnotesize
Compare 5.B3: for a smooth function a Chebyshev basis converges \emph{geometrically}. The gap between $1/n$ and $r^{-n}$ is the whole argument.
\end{examplebox}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vspace{1mm}
\src{Quant\_Macro Lec.~9, ``Trigonometric series''.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why This Is a Macroeconomics Problem}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
\small
Gibbs is usually filed under Fourier series, but the phenomenon is about \textbf{smooth global bases meeting non-smooth functions}, and polynomial bases have it too.
\medskip

Macroeconomic policy and value functions are full of non-smoothness:
\begin{itemize}\small
  \item \textbf{credit constraints} --- a kink exactly at the borrowing limit (2.A1, 2.A2);
  \item \textbf{irreversible investment} --- a kink at zero;
  \item \textbf{discrete choice} --- an outright jump.
\end{itemize}
\medskip
A Chebyshev expansion will overshoot near each of them, and \textbf{raising the order does not fix it}. It confines the oscillation to a smaller neighbourhood of the kink, at a larger amplitude nearby.
\end{column}
\begin{column}{0.42\textwidth}
\begin{conceptbox}[title=\textbf{Two ways out}]\small
\textbf{Piecewise} bases --- put an element boundary at the kink and the problem disappears. That is 5.B4.
\medskip
\textbf{Adaptive} bases --- let the approximant learn where to bend. That is what a neural network does, and it is S7.
\end{conceptbox}
\medskip
\begin{cautionbox}\footnotesize
And it is why the Aiyagari economy is not solved with a global polynomial.
\end{cautionbox}
\end{column}
\end{columns}
\vspace{1mm}
\src{Quant\_Macro Lec.~9, ``Gibbs Phenomenon in Polynomial Approximation''.}
\end{frame}

%=============================================================================
\begin{frame}{Takeaways}
\begin{takeawaybox}
\begin{itemize}\small
  \item Two independent choices: a \textbf{global} basis (spectral) or a \textbf{local} one (finite elements), and separately how to project.
  \item Monomials are complete by Stone--Weierstrass and \textbf{unusable} in practice: the least-squares matrix is the Hilbert matrix, with condition number $\mathbf{3\times10^{18}}$ at degree $13$ --- no correct digits, and no error message.
  \item An orthogonal basis fixes it completely. The Chebyshev interpolation matrix has condition number $\mathbf{1.414}$ at \emph{every} degree tested, from $5$ to $21$.
  \item Orthogonality also makes coefficients into simple inner products, $c_i=\langle x,v_i\rangle/\|v_i\|^{2}$ --- no system to solve, and each coefficient independently meaningful.
  \item Trigonometric bases suit periodic problems; economics has none. Forcing them gives $O(1/n)$ convergence, not the geometric rate a smooth problem deserves.
  \item \textbf{Gibbs does not go away with more terms}: measured at $18.8\%$ with three terms and $17.90\%$ with fifty-one, converging to $17.898\%$. Kinks need piecewise or adaptive bases, not higher order.
\end{itemize}
\end{takeawaybox}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Bridge: The Basis That Wins}
\begin{columns}[T]
\begin{column}{0.53\textwidth}
\small
We now have the requirements: orthogonal, well conditioned at any degree, defined on an interval rather than a circle, and fast for smooth functions.
\medskip

One family satisfies all of them, and it is the default in this literature for good measured reasons --- including a theorem saying you cannot do much better with \emph{any} polynomial of the same degree.
