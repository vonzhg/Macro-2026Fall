\section*{B2. Solve the calibrated model three ways (25 points)}

Use the stochastic model of A1 at your calibration, a 7-state chain from B1(b), and capital on
$[0.5k^*,1.5k^*]$.

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=*]

\item \textbf{(7 pts) A global solution.} Solve with your best solver from B1(a). Report the grid,
tolerance, iterations and wall time, and check that the policy crosses the 45-degree line near the
analytical $k^*$ in the middle productivity state.

\solution{\keyBthree}

\item \textbf{(6 pts) First-order perturbation.} \emph{(Session 5, Block A.)} Log-linearize, solve by
QZ (or any method from 5.A4), and state the Blanchard--Kahn count you verified.

\solution{\keyBfour}

\item \textbf{(7 pts) Chebyshev collocation.} \emph{(Session 5, Block B.)} Approximate consumption with
a degree-7 Chebyshev polynomial in $k$ for each productivity state, collocate the Euler residual at
the Chebyshev nodes, and warm-start from (b).

\solution{\keyBfive}

\item \textbf{(5 pts) Accuracy against cost, and a decision.} One table for the three methods: numbers
stored, solve time, and $\log_{10}$ of the maximum and mean unit-free Euler residual at 1,000 random
off-grid points over the whole grid, and over $[0.95k^*,1.05k^*]$. Submit your best maximum
$\log_{10}$ residual, with its grid, tolerance and wall time, to the leaderboard. Then decide:
\textbf{which method is fit for the positive question, and which for the normative one?} Be precise
about the normative question: what does a first-order solution give if you also approximate
\emph{welfare} to first order (certainty equivalence, Session 5), and what if instead you evaluate
exact utility along the first-order \emph{policy}? Which is the right way to use it, and why?

\solution{\keyBsix}

\end{enumerate}

\section*{B3. The answers (20 points)}

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*), leftmargin=*]

\item \textbf{(10 pts) The positive answer.} Simulate the model, HP-filter the logs of $Y$, $C$ and
$I$, and put the six moments next to \textbf{your} data table from A3(a). Report them twice: from
one long simulation, and as the mean and standard deviation across 200 simulated samples of the
\emph{same length as your data}. Which comparison is the right one, and why? Then answer the
question: what fraction of the U.S.\ standard deviation of output (and of its variance) do TFP
shocks account for in this model? Name the moment the model gets most wrong and the model feature
most likely responsible. How much does switching to the Tauchen chain move your answer?

\solution{\keyBseven}

\item \textbf{(10 pts) The normative answer.} Define the welfare cost of fluctuations as the
percentage $\lambda$ by which consumption in every date and state of the stochastic economy must be
raised to make the household as well off as in a deterministic economy where $z$ is fixed at
$\bar z$, both starting at the deterministic steady state $\bar k^*$:
\[
  \mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_t\beta^t\ln\big((1+\lambda)c_t\big)\Big]=\frac{\ln \bar c^*}{1-\beta}.
\]
Compute $\lambda$ from a global solution, averaging over the ergodic distribution of $z$, for two
comparison economies: $\bar z=1$, which preserves the mean of $\ln z$, and
$\bar z=\mathbb{E}[z]$, which preserves the mean of $z$. Which comparison is fair, and why do the
two differ? Compare your answer with Lucas's benchmark $\tfrac12\mathrm{var}(\ln c)$. Interpret the \emph{size}: is it large or
small, and what does that imply for stabilization policy in this model? Interpret the \emph{sign}:
could it be negative here, and what property of the technology would make it so? Finally, using
your answer to A1(d), explain in one paragraph why an economy with uninsurable idiosyncratic risk can
give a much larger number --- the question Homework 2 takes up.

\solution{\keyBeight}

\end{enumerate}

\section*{B6. Choose your route (20 points)}

Research projects differ in where their risk lies. Pick \textbf{one} route and go deeper; the route
you choose is a component your final project can reuse. Say in your charter which route you chose
and why.

\begin{description}[leftmargin=*, style=nextline]
\item[R1. The numerical frontier (Sessions 4--5).] Solve the calibrated model by
\textbf{second-order perturbation} and recompute the welfare cost of B3(b) with it. How close is it
to the welfare cost from the first-order and the global \emph{policies}, each evaluated by exact
utility? Explain the size of the differences you find (think envelope theorem). Simulate the second-order
solution with and without \textbf{pruning} and report whether it matters at your calibration. Then
either raise risk (triple $\sigma$) until perturbation visibly fails, or solve the endogenous-labour
model of the bonus on a two-dimensional \textbf{Smolyak} grid or with finite elements.
\item[R2. Data and estimation (Sessions 3--4).] Replace the calibration of $\rho$ and $\sigma$ by
\textbf{simulated method of moments}: choose at least three moments from your A3(a) table, a
weighting matrix, and a simulation size, and \emph{first} show that your estimator recovers known
parameters from data simulated by the model. Then estimate on the U.S.\ moments, report standard
errors, and test the overidentifying restriction. Does the model fit what you asked it to fit?
\item[R3. Policy (Session 2).] Add a proportional tax $\tau_k$ on capital income, rebated lump
sum. Derive the steady-state effect of $\tau_k$ on $k^*$ and $c^*$, and state the Ramsey
steady-state tax in this model (Chamley--Judd). Compute the welfare effect, \emph{including the
transition}, of a permanent surprise move from $\tau_k=0$ to $\tau_k=0.25$ starting at the old
steady state. Explain why the steady-state comparison and the transition-inclusive answer differ,
and what a government without commitment (Session 2, deck 2.B3) would be tempted to do.
\end{description}

\solution{\keyBtwelve}
